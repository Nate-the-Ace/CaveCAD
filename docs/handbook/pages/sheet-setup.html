Sheet Setup

A docked palette for building a sheet, and a rough picture of it before you commit. sheet


The preview, redrawn as you tick: paper, the cave's footprint, the furniture, and the elevation on its own sheet.

When you reach for it

When the map is drawn and has to become paper.

Steps

  1. Pick the paper. ANSI and ARCH first, ISO A4 to A0 after. It is used the way up it is listed (landscape), always; tick Portrait, beside the paper and scale, to turn it. The sheet size never changes by itself, whatever the scale or wherever you slide the viewport.
  2. Take the proposed scale or change it. It offers the most detailed standard plot scale the plan fits at. Scales run 1" = 10 ft up to 1" = 500 ft, then the metric ratios 1:100 to 1:2000. A metric sheet gets a bar counted in metres and captioned with its ratio.
  3. Tick what goes on the sheet: border, scale bar, north arrow, title block, and the extended elevation on a second sheet. The preview redraws as you tick — the paper, the cave's footprint on it, the furniture, and where the elevation bands land.
  4. Arrange the page, if the default does not suit it. Drag the title block, the scale bar, the north arrow or the cave itself around the preview. Edges snap to the dashed margin, to the paper and to each other; middles snap to middles, so a piece can be centred left to right, top to bottom, or both. A magenta line shows an edge snap and a blue one a centring, and the panel says which. Reset Layout puts everything back.
  5. Build Sheet writes it. One sheet per file: <Cave> Plan Sheet.dxf, and <Cave> Profile Sheet.dxf when the elevation is ticked.

Two norths on one arrow

The map is drawn in true north — every azimuth was rotated by its trip's declination as it was drawn. The compass in your hand underground is not, so the arrow carries a second arm at magnetic north from the same origin: the angle between the arms is the declination. It is drawn shorter and in grey, labelled mN against the true arrow's N, so the two are never read as one symbol.

The arm uses the declination of the latest trip in the survey, and the caption says which and when — MAGNETIC NORTH 3.2° E (2024-11-03). Declination drifts about a degree every few years, so an undated magnetic north is a number with a shelf life and no label on it. A drawing with no survey in it gets the true arrow and no magnetic claim — and neither does one whose trips all record a declination of 0.0, which is the suite's default for a trip nobody has told rather than a measurement. Record a real declination on a trip and the arm appears.

It is one piece of furniture: drag the north arrow and both arms, both labels and the caption move together.

Dragging, and what moves

A drag is remembered in inches of paper, not drawing units, so a bar nudged two inches to the right stays two inches to the right when you step the scale or change the paper.

Dragging the cave is the odd one out: the cave does not move. Its coordinates are survey data, so the paper slides under it instead — which looks the same on screen and leaves the survey alone. The furniture travels with the paper.

A chunked elevation (see Vertical caves) is the other odd one, the other way: it does not travel as one piece. Every chunk gets its own box in the preview, labelled the same as its pitch-depth caption, and each drags independently — arrange the shafts of a pit sheet the way they read best on paper, not only the way the auto-layout stacked them. Like every other drag on this page, it is sheet-only: rearranging chunks here does not change the live drawing's own chunked elevation, and reopening Sheet Setup later starts the chunks back at their auto-layout row. An extended or projected elevation is unaffected — those still travel as the one piece they have always been.

Centring beats lining up. A page's midline nearly always has some other piece's edge near it, so a midline within reach takes the drag even when an edge is nearer — otherwise a bar you meant to centre lands a hair off it, which looks exactly like being on it.

The dashed rectangle is the margin: half an inch of paper at every edge, kept clear so the sheet can be bound, trimmed and held — about as wide as a plotter's own unprintable border. It is a guide and is never drawn on the sheet itself. The furniture sits on that line.

Scanned sketches never reach a sheet

Not a checkbox, a rule. A scanned page is a tracing reference; everything worth keeping off it has already been traced. On a plotted sheet it is a photograph of somebody's handwriting printed under the map. The rule is the entity, not the layer, so a sheet carries no raster at all — the aerial photograph included.

Plot it to PDF

Export PDF plots every sheet the last Build Sheet wrote, one file each, beside its DXF: <Cave> Plan Sheet.pdf and, with the elevation ticked, <Cave> Profile Sheet.pdf. Open a sheet file yourself and press it with nothing built, and it plots that one.

Each PDF opens in your system's default PDF reader as soon as it is made.

The page is the sheet's paper at the sheet's scale. Build Sheet writes the paper size, orientation, print scale and offset into the file, so File > Print plots it correctly too, with no trip through Page Setup. The PDF's own page size is read back and compared to the paper you chose; a mismatch is reported rather than shipped. If the PDF already exists you are asked before it is replaced.

One PDF per sheet. A cave too big for one sheet still needs a larger paper or a smaller scale; splitting a sheet across pages is not done.

A cave bigger than one sheet

Pick a scale the cave will not fit on one sheet at, and the preview does not complain: it lays out more sheets. The sheet you place stays where it is on the screen. The blue box is the viewport, the map at this scale: drag it over the sheet, and wherever it runs past the sheet's margin, adjacent sheets appear beside it to carry the rest, and go again when it slides back. Every sheet is shown as the part of the map it prints; only the sheet you placed is drawn as paper, with the title block, scale bar and north arrow on it. The viewport's edges snap to the sheets' edges and match lines. Change the scale or the paper and the grid is laid out again.

Sheets are numbered by grid: rows lettered from the north, columns numbered from the west. A1 is the north-west sheet, A2 is east of it, B1 is south of it. A square of the grid the cave never touches is not built, but keeps its place in the numbering, so an L-shaped cave makes an L of sheets and sheet B3 is always where B3 says.

Neighbouring sheets meet along a match line, drawn dashed on both. Each sheet prints, along every match line, MATCH LINE - SEE SHEET B2 naming the sheet that continues it, and its own name, SHEET A1, at the top left. Each sheet also repeats half an inch of its neighbour past the line, so a passage that crosses it reads continuously and two printed sheets can be lined up by eye.

Build writes one file per sheet, <Cave> Plan Sheet A1.dxf, ... A2.dxf and so on. Export PDF writes a single PDF for the whole plan set, <Cave> Plan Sheets.pdf, one page per sheet in grid order (A1 first), and opens it. The profile sheet keeps a PDF of its own.

Every sheet is map right out to the page margin. Nothing is kept clear for the sheet's own elements. Sheet A1 carries the title block; every sheet carries a scale bar and a north arrow. Each element has a white backing: wherever you have slid the viewport so that the map runs under one, the map is cut away behind it, so the element reads clean. (It is cut away rather than painted over, because a plot prints a white fill as black.) Drag the elements themselves if you want them somewhere else; the same arrangement is used on every sheet.

Only a sheet's own map area shows on it. Anything that belongs to a neighbour is clipped away: what lies wholly outside is dropped, and a wall that crosses the edge is cut exactly at it, so nothing prints in the margin. Plan sheets from an earlier layout (a longer grid, or the single sheet it used to be) are left in the folder and named in the build message so they are not mistaken for part of the set.

Everything starts inside the margin

The title block, scale bar and north arrow are placed by their real size, captions included, so the whole of each sits inside the dashed margin and none sits on another. On a sheet wide enough they share one row, with the north arrow against the right margin. On a narrow or turned sheet they wrap onto a second row, and the band under the map grows to hold them. The scale bar's numbers are kept above the bottom margin. Elevation bands start inside the margin on every paper size.

When it refuses

  • It says plainly when something is off the paper, and which thing.
  • Unticking the elevation removes it from the sheet rather than leaving it unplaced — it would otherwise sit a thousand feet below the plan, outside the border.
